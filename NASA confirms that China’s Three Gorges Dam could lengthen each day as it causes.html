NASA scientists have made a remarkable discovery regarding China’s Three Gorges Dam. The massive hydroelectric structure is actually altering Earth’s rotation in a way that makes our days fractionally longer. This scientific finding reveals the surprising planetary impact of one of humanity’s largest engineering achievements.

How China’s Three Gorges Dam impacts Earth’s rotation

The Three Gorges Dam, standing 175 meters above sea level in western Wuhan, represents a marvel of modern engineering. According to NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center geophysicist Dr. Benjamin Fong Chao, this colossal structure influences our planet in unexpected ways. When filled to capacity with approximately 10 trillion gallons of water, the dam increases Earth’s day length by 0.06 microseconds.

This phenomenon relates to the physical principle known as moment of inertia. By redistributing such enormous water mass, the dam subtly affects Earth’s mass distribution and consequently its rotation speed. Similar effects have been observed with major geological events like the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake, which shortened days by 2.68 microseconds by shifting tectonic plates.

Scientists studying celestial phenomena have documented various impacts on Earth’s timekeeping. Just as while observing the Moon, these scientists were stunned by rapid canyon formations, researchers tracking Earth’s rotation have identified multiple natural and artificial factors affecting our planet’s movement.

The Three Gorges Dam doesn’t just alter day length. Research indicates it can make Earth slightly flatter by shifting the North and South Poles approximately two centimeters (0.8 inches). While seemingly minor, these changes demonstrate how human structures can influence planetary physics.

The world’s largest power station and its environmental implications

As the world’s largest power station, the Three Gorges Dam generates an impressive 0.54 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity daily. This output can power 5.4 million households for an entire month when operating at full capacity. The Chinese government invested ¥203 billion ($31.765 billion) in this megastructure, considering its energy production capabilities worth the substantial cost.

However, the dam’s construction required significant sacrifice. The project flooded 13 cities and 140 towns, displacing more than 31 million people. Its reservoir flood storage capacity reaches 22 km³, creating a massive artificial lake that permanently altered the regional landscape.

China continues pursuing ambitious engineering projects, including plans for a space-based power station of comparable scale and approval for constructing a dam three times larger than Three Gorges on the Yarlung Tsangpo River. These developments reflect growing global interest in megastructures despite their environmental implications.

Climate scientists have been monitoring various planetary changes, including research published in Nature examining 120 years of data. The study “Contributions of core, mantle and climatological processes to Earth’s polar motion” suggests climate change has independently lengthened our days by approximately 1.33 milliseconds per century since 2000.

Megastructures and their planetary effects

The Three Gorges Dam joins other remarkable human-made structures altering our planet. Projects like the Great Wall of China, Beijing Daxing International Airport, and the Burj Khalifa demonstrate humanity’s capacity to create increasingly massive constructions. Even the International Space Station, though not Earth-bound, represents this trend toward building at unprecedented scales.

Astronomers and planetary scientists continue examining how both natural and artificial forces influence Earth and other celestial bodies. Recent advances in observational technology have expanded our understanding of cosmic phenomena, including potential discoveries in our own solar system. Researchers using new telescopes may soon reveal evidence of a ninth planet in our solar system.

Despite the Three Gorges Dam’s measurable impact on Earth’s rotation, the practical effect remains minimal. The 0.06 microsecond daily extension amounts to roughly three days over the entire age of the universe. Online reactions to this discovery have included humorous comments about extended workdays, but the scientific reality presents no cause for concern regarding noticeable time changes in human experience.

As humanity continues building increasingly ambitious structures, scientists will monitor their cumulative effects on our planet’s physical properties. The Three Gorges Dam demonstrates how human engineering can influence even fundamental planetary characteristics like rotation speed.
